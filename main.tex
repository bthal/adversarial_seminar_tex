\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Over the past decade, deep neural networks have become the dominant approach to a broad range of perception tasks from image classification and speech recognition to natural language understanding in many cases matching or surpassing human performance on standard benchmarks.\cite{lecun2015deep, he2015delving} On the strength of these results, such models are increasingly deployed in consequential, safety-relevant settings, including autonomous driving,\cite{grigorescu2020survey} biometric identification,\cite{schroff2015facenet} and automated content moderation.\cite{gorwa2020algorithmic}

This success, however, rests on a surprisingly fragile foundation. In 2013, Szegedy et al.\ \cite{szegedy2013intriguing} showed that state-of-the-art classifiers can be fooled by \textit{adversarial examples}: inputs altered by a perturbation so small that it is imperceptible to a human, yet enough to make the model produce a confident but incorrect prediction. The most widely known illustration adds a small, carefully produced pertubation to a photograph of a panda and leads a high-accuracy network to confidently label it as a gibbon (Figure~\ref{fig:panda_gibbon}). Such examples are neither rare accidents nor quirks of a single model: the same perturbed input frequently fools different architectures trained on different data, and the vulnerability appears across essentially all kinds of machine learners.\cite{goodfellow2014explaining, biggio2018wild}

In this paper, we are examining closely, not only how the properties of adversarial attacks where unravelled as different strategies to systematically and efficiently produce adversarial systems. We also turn to the question of how the topic of adversarial attacks shaped the discussion on \textit{robustness} in the machine learning domain in general. We come to the conclusion that adversarial examples have fundamentally reshaped how computer scientists think about machine learning models: they demonstrate that high test accuracy does not imply that a network has grasped the concepts a human would, and they expose a fascinating mismatch between human and machine perception. \textit{Robustness} has thus become a property that must be explicitly defined, demanded, and asserted.\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}

This report is intended as a broad introduction to the topic. In our examination of specific attack methods, we thus concentrate on the classical setting of evasion and poisoning attacks against image classifiers, surveying the ideas and methods that have shaped the research area.

The rest of the report is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:attacks} defines adversarial examples precisely, introduces a taxonomy that classifies attacks along four orthogonal dimensions, and reviews the most influential evasion and poisoning attacks. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} then contains our discussion: the pertinence of adversarial attacks in the real and physical world, the competing attempts to explain why adversarial examples exist in the first place, and a reflection on what makes the phenomenon a worthwhile object of study.




\section{Adversarial Attacks}
\subsection{Definition}
\textit{Adversarial Attacks} can be described as inputting \textit{adversarial examples} into a machine learning system.\cite{brendel2017decision} These two terms where introduced by \cite{szegedy2013intriguing} and have since been used in different variations, however we here use the following definition:

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{panda_gibbon.png}
    \caption{Classic illustration of an adversarial example. A small perturbation that is nearly imperceptible to humans is added to an image of a panda. While the original image is correctly classified as a panda with high confidence, the perturbed image is misclassified as a gibbon; also with high confidence. Adapted from \cite{goodfellow2014explaining}.}
    \label{fig:panda_gibbon}
\end{figure}

\begin{definition}[Adversarial Example]
An \textit{adversarial example} is an \textbf{input to a machine learning model} that has been \textbf{intentionally perturbed}, typically \textbf{in a subtle and often imperceptible manner}, such that the model produces an incorrect prediction while the semantic content of the input remains unchanged.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Machine Learning Models.}
While adversarial examples are most commonly studied in the context of deep neural network classifiers,\cite{szegedy2013intriguing,madry2017towards,pitropakis2019taxonomy} the phenomenon is not limited to neural networks. Adversarial vulnerabilities have been demonstrated across a wide range of machine learning architectures, including decision trees, support vector machines, $k$-nearest neighbour classifiers, logistic regression models, ensemble methods and more. This suggests that susceptibility to adversarial manipulation is a general property of learned decision functions rather than a characteristic of a specific machine learning method.\cite{papernot2016distillation, szegedy2013intriguing, goodfellow2014explaining}

\paragraph{Intentional Perturbations.}
The requirement that perturbations are \textit{intentional} reflects the modern understanding of adversarial examples. The seminal work of Szegedy et al. \cite{szegedy2013intriguing} demonstrated the existence of inputs that caused misclassification despite appearing unchanged to humans, but did not initially frame these inputs primarily as the result of a deliberate attack. As the field evolved, researchers showed that such inputs can be generated systematically through optimization procedures and exploited by an adversary.\cite{moosavi2016deepfool, madry2017towards} Consequently, adversarial examples are now typically discussed in the context of adversarial attacks, where perturbations are deliberately crafted to induce erroneous model behaviour.\cite{chakraborty2018adversarial, pitropakis2019taxonomy, costa2024deep}

\paragraph{Subtle and Imperceptible Perturbations.}
The notion of \textit{subtle} perturbations can be interpreted in two related but distinct ways. First, adversarial perturbations are commonly constrained to be mathematically small according to a chosen distance metric. The most frequently used measures are the $L_0$ norm, which counts the number of modified features or pixels; the $L_2$ norm, which measures the Euclidean distance between the original and perturbed inputs; and the $L_\infty$ norm, which limits the maximum change applied to any individual feature.\cite{costa2024deep} These norms define the perturbation budget available to an adversary and provide a mathematical notion of similarity between inputs. We will discuss these different norms in more detail in subsubsection~\ref{sssec:norms}.

Second, perturbations are often required to be imperceptible to humans. In the image domain, this means that the perturbed input should be visually indistinguishable or at least hardly distinguishable from the original image, preserving its semantic meaning.\cite{madry2017towards,moosavi2016deepfool} Although we rarely see the perceptibility of the applied perturbations rigorously validated it is nevertheless very common for authors to state, based on a visual assessment, that the adversarial examples they have generated are indistinguishable from the original to humans.\cite{brendel2017decision, chakraborty2018adversarial, costa2024deep, liang2022adversarial, papernot2016distillation, papernot2016transferability}

\paragraph{Confidence of Misclassification.}
Some definitions additionally require adversarial examples to induce misclassifications with high confidence.\cite{madry2017towards} In practice, however, high-confidence errors are often a consequence of the optimization objective used during adversarial example generation rather than a defining characteristic of adversarial examples themselves. For example, early optimization-based attacks explicitly maximize the model loss or the probability assigned to a target class.\cite{szegedy2013intriguing} Consequently, contemporary literature generally treats high confidence as an attack objective rather than a necessary component of the definition.

\subsection{Taxonomy}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

Adversarial attacks can be categorised according to several orthogonal dimensions, including the attacker's knowledge of the target model, the stage at which the attack is performed, the attacker's objective, and the constraint that bounds the perturbation. These dimensions are widely used throughout the literature and provide a useful framework for comparing attack methodologies.\cite{pitropakis2019taxonomy,costa2024deep} Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy} summarises these four dimensions together with their possible values; the attacks discussed in this survey are classified according to this taxonomy, with a complete overview provided in Table~\ref{table_with_all_attacks}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_taxonomy.pdf}

A given attack is characterised by one choice from each column.}
    \label{fig:taxonomy}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{By Attacker Knowledge}

One of the most common distinctions is based on the amount of information available to the attacker about the target model.\cite{pitropakis2019taxonomy,costa2024deep}

\paragraph{White-box Attacks.}
In a white-box setting, the attacker has complete knowledge of the target model, including its architecture, parameters, training procedure, and gradients. This level of access enables highly effective optimisation-based attacks that directly exploit gradient information to identify adversarial perturbations. Many influential attacks, including the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM), Projected Gradient Descent (PGD), and the Carlini--Wagner attack, were originally proposed in this setting. White-box attacks are commonly used to evaluate the worst-case robustness of machine learning models.

\paragraph{Black-box Attacks.}
In a black-box setting, the attacker has no direct access to the internal structure of the model and can only observe its outputs in response to supplied inputs. Such attacks more closely resemble many real-world scenarios, where proprietary models are exposed only through prediction interfaces or APIs. Black-box attacks typically rely on repeated queries, surrogate models, or the transferability of adversarial examples between different models.

\subsubsection{By Attack Stage}

Attacks can also be classified according to the stage of the machine learning pipeline at which they are carried out.\cite{chakraborty2018adversarial,pitropakis2019taxonomy}

\paragraph{Evasion Attacks.}
Evasion attacks are performed during the inference phase after a model has been trained. The attacker modifies input samples in order to induce misclassification while avoiding detection. Since they require no influence over the training process, evasion attacks are often considered the most practical and widely studied form of adversarial attack. Examples include adversarial images that cause image classifiers to produce incorrect predictions and manipulated network traffic designed to evade intrusion detection systems.

\paragraph{Poisoning Attacks.}
Poisoning attacks target the training phase by injecting maliciously crafted samples into the training dataset. The objective may be to degrade the model's overall performance, manipulate its behaviour for specific inputs, or implant hidden backdoors that can later be triggered by an attacker. While poisoning attacks require some influence over the data collection or training pipeline, this assumption is realistic in settings that rely on crowdsourced, user-generated, or automatically collected data. Examples include recommendation systems trained on user feedback, spam filters trained on submitted emails, and machine learning models trained on large datasets scraped from the internet.

\subsubsection{By Attack Goal}

A third dimension considers the attacker's intended outcome.\cite{chakraborty2018adversarial,costa2024deep,brendel2017decision}

\paragraph{Targeted Attacks.}
Targeted attacks seek to force the model to produce a specific attacker-chosen output. For example, an attacker may attempt to modify an image of a panda such that it is classified as a gibbon. Because the attack must drive the prediction toward a particular target class, targeted attacks are generally more difficult to perform than untargeted attacks.

\paragraph{Untargeted Attacks.}
Untargeted attacks aim only to cause a prediction error, but do not specify the resulting incorrect class. The attack is therefore successful as soon as the model's prediction differs from the correct label. The attacker might aim at having the classifier recognize the image as showing anything but a panda.

\subsubsection{By Perturbation Constraint}
\label{sssec:norms}
Adversarial attacks are commonly categorised according to the distance metric used to constrain the perturbation applied to an input.\cite{costa2024deep, liang2022adversarial} These constraints define what constitutes a \textit{small} modification and therefore determine the attacker's search space.\cite{carlini2017towards} The majority of attacks in the literature operate under one of three norm-based threat models. Figure~\ref{fig:norms} illustrates these three norms geometrically.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{norms.png}
    \caption{Geometric interpretation of the three norm constraints commonly used in adversarial machine learning. The unit balls of the $L_0$, $L_2$, and $L_\infty$ norms are shown in a two-dimensional feature space. The $L_0$ norm constrains the number of modified features, the $L_2$ norm constrains the Euclidean distance between the original and perturbed input, and the $L_\infty$ norm constrains the maximum change applied to any individual feature. Figure adapted from \cite{costa2024deep}.}
    \label{fig:norms}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{$L_0$-Constrained Attacks.}
The $L_0$ norm measures the number of features that have been modified. In the context of image classification, this corresponds to the number of altered pixels regardless of the magnitude of the changes. Attacks operating under an $L_0$ constraint aim to minimise the number of modified features while still causing misclassification.\cite{costa2024deep, liang2022adversarial} Examples for such attacks include Deepfool \cite{moosavi2016deepfool} and Carlini \& Wagner.\cite{carlini2017towards}

\paragraph{$L_2$-Constrained Attacks.}
The $L_2$ norm measures the Euclidean distance between the original and perturbed input vectors. Under this constraint, perturbations may be distributed across many features as long as the overall magnitude remains small.\cite{costa2024deep, liang2022adversarial} The seminal work of Szegedy et al. \cite{szegedy2013intriguing} formulated adversarial example generation as an optimisation problem under an $L_2$ constraint. Due to its intuitive geometric interpretation, the $L_2$ norm remains widely used when evaluating adversarial robustness.\cite{costa2024deep}

\paragraph{$L_\infty$-Constrained Attacks.}
The $L_\infty$ norm limits the maximum change that can be applied to any individual feature.\cite{costa2024deep, liang2022adversarial} In image domains, this ensures that every pixel is modified by at most a small amount. Many influential attacks, including \textit{FGSM} and \textit{PGD}, operate under an $L_\infty$ constraint because it provides a straightforward approximation of perceptual similarity while enabling efficient optimisation.\cite{goodfellow2014explaining,madry2017towards}

\subsubsection{L-BFGS}
The first widely recognised adversarial attack was introduced by Szegedy et al.~\cite{szegedy2013intriguing}. Their work did not only show that machine learning models are vulnerable to inputs that lead to incorrect predictions while appearing unchanged to human observers, first describing the phenomenon of an adversarial example. They further introduced a fairly reliable method to find such examples.

To generate adversarial examples, the authors formulated the problem as a constrained optimisation task. Given an input $x$ and a target class $l$, the objective is to find the smallest perturbation $r$ such that the perturbed input $x+r$ is classified as the target class:

\begin{equation}
\min_r \|r\|_2
\quad \text{subject to} \quad
f(x+r)=l,
\end{equation}

where $f$ denotes the classifier and $\|r\|_2$ represents the Euclidean distance between the original and perturbed input. 
Directly solving this problem is difficult because the classification constraint $f(x+r)=l$ is highly non-linear and non-convex. To make the problem tractable, Szegedy et al. reformulated it as a continuous optimisation problem by replacing the hard classification constraint with a differentiable loss term that encourages the model to predict the target class. Specifically, they minimise

\begin{equation}
\min_r \; c\|r\|_2 + \mathrm{loss}_f(x+r,l),
\end{equation}

subject to the box constraint

\begin{equation}
x+r \in [0,1]^m,
\end{equation}

where $\mathrm{loss}_f(x+r,l)$ measures how strongly the network predicts class $l$, $c>0$ controls the trade-off between achieving the target misclassification and keeping the perturbation small, and the box constraint ensures that the perturbed input remains a valid image. This reformulation yields a differentiable objective whose gradients can be computed via backpropagation, allowing the perturbation to be found efficiently using the box-constrained Limited-memory Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno (L-BFGS) optimisation algorithm. A line search over $c$ is then performed to identify the smallest successful perturbation that causes the classifier to output the target class.

Although the L-BFGS attack is still computationally expensive compared to later methods, its significance lies in establishing the existence of adversarial examples and motivating the extensive research that followed. For this reason, it is widely regarded as the starting point of adversarial machine learning research.\cite{goodfellow2014explaining, costa2024deep, liang2022adversarial}

\subsubsection{DeepFool}
 DeepFool was introduced by Moosavi-Dezfooli et al.~\cite{moosavi2016deepfool} in 2016. The central idea of this iterative is to characterize adversarial vulnerability geometrically: rather than loosely optimizing an attack loss, DeepFool estimates the distance of an input to the closest decision boundary. For affine classifiers, this distance could be calculated in a closed form by projecting the input point onto the decision hyperplane. The highly nonlinear decision boundaries of deep neural networks are addressed by DeepFool by exploiting the observation that although the true boundary of a neural network may be highly curved, sufficiently small neighborhoods can often be approximated accurately by a hyperplane; the shortest path to this approximate boundary is assumed to provide a good estimate of the minimal adversarial perturbation. This auxiliary hyperplane is recomputed at each iteration from a first-order Taylor expansion of the classifier around the current point; as the point is repeatedly displaced onto the latest hyperplane, it moves closer and closer to the true, nonlinear decision boundary until it eventually crosses it. In mathematical terms, at an intermediate point $x_i$, the classifier is iteratively replaced by its local linear approximation,                                   

\begin{equation}
    f(x) \approx f(x_i) + \nabla f(x_i)^T(x-x_i),
\end{equation}

which defines a locally linear decision boundary. The perturbation is obtained by projecting the current point onto this locally estimated boundary, yielding an update

\begin{equation}
    x_{i+1}=x_i+r_i,
\end{equation}

where $r_i$ is the smallest perturbation that crosses the linearized boundary. The procedure is repeated until the classifier changes its prediction.

For multiclass classifiers, DeepFool extends this idea by considering the set of pairwise decision boundaries between the current class and competing classes. At each iteration, the method computes the locally closest boundary under the linear approximation and updates the input toward that boundary. Figure~\ref{fig:deepfoolmulticlasslinearapproximation} visualises this iterative step.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{deepfool_multiclass_linear_approximation}
	\caption{The approach of DeepFool estimates the position of the decision boundaries with the other classes with a linear representation using first-order Taylor expansion. By iteratively projecting the input point onto this auxilary hyperplane, the pertubed point scooches closer and closer to the actual decision boundary of the model until eventually crossing it. }
	\label{fig:deepfoolmulticlasslinearapproximation}
\end{figure}


DeepFool is made several important contributions to the field of adversarial attacks, the first major one being that it provided a practical approximation of minimal adversarial perturbations, making adversarial robustness palpable in terms of distance to the decision boundary. Secondly, it introduced a geometric perspective on adversarial attacks: instead of treating adversarial generation as a generic constrained optimization problem, it directly exploits the local structure of the classifier. Third, this formulation leads to substantially improved efficiency compared with previous optimization-based attacks such as the L-BFGS attack of Szegedy et al.~\cite{szegedy2013intriguing}, which required solving a computationally expensive optimization problem for each input. By using gradient information to directly estimate the boundary geometry, DeepFool achieves smaller perturbations than earlier fast gradient-based methods while requiring considerably less computation than general-purpose optimization approaches.

\subsubsection{Carlini-Wagner}
The Carlini-Wagner (C\&W) attack is an influential attack presented by Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2017towards}. The method is building directly upon the original L-BFGS method by Szegedy et al.~\cite{szegedy2013intriguing} by substantially improving the objective function of the optimization problem. They further introduced a trick to formulate the problem as an unconstrained optimization task, instead of a constrained one.

Just like L-BFGS, given an input example $x$ and a target class $t$, the attack seeks an adversarial example $x'=x+\delta$ by solving:

\begin{equation}
\min_{\delta} \|\delta\|_p + c \cdot f(x+\delta),
\end{equation}

 of a logit-based loss function rather than optimizing the softmax output probabilities. The loss is defined as:

\begin{equation}
f(x') =
\max \left(
\max_{i \neq t} Z_i(x') - Z_t(x'),
-k
\right),
\end{equation}

where $Z_i(x')$ denotes the logit corresponding to class $i$, and $k$ is a confidence parameter controlling the margin by which the target class must exceed competing classes. This formulation directly optimizes against the decision boundary of the classifier and thus efficiently calculates adversarial examples.

To address the pixel-range constraint of image inputs, the C\&W attack introduces a clever variable transformation:

\begin{equation}
x'=\frac{1}{2}(\tanh(w)+1),
\end{equation}

where $w$ is an unconstrained latent space optimization variable. This transformation converts the original constrained optimization problem into an unconstrained one while guaranteeing that the generated adversarial example remains within the valid input range. The attack then optimizes $w$ using gradient-based optimization methods, typically the Adam optimizer\cite{kingma2014adam}.

The original work introduced variants based on different distance metrics, including $L_0$, $L_2$, and $L_\infty$ norms, allowing the attack to evaluate robustness under different threat models.

The effectiveness of the C\&W attack also demonstrated weaknesses in several proposed defenses. In particular, it was shown to successfully bypass defense distillation \cite{papernot2016distillation}, a technique proposed to improve adversarial robustness by training a model using softened output probabilities from a teacher network. Although defense distillation appeared to increase resistance against earlier attacks, Carlini and Wagner demonstrated that the defense distillation method did in fact only obscure the model's gradient albeit not truly hiding it. By directly targetting the logit with their loss function, Carlini et al. were able to efficiently generate adversarial examples in spite of defense distillation by directly approximating the obscured decision boundary.

\subsubsection{Transfer Attacks}
In a transfer attack, an adversary first obtains a substitute model that he has full access to as a stand-in for a target black-box model. On this substitute, white-box attacks can be performed to find adversarial examples. Since they generally transfer from one model to another, these can then be used to attack the actual target model.

\subsubsection{Jacobian-based dataset augmentation}
Papernot et al. \cite{papernot2017practical} introduced black-box attacks via transfer attacks where neither target model parameters nor a suitable data set is accessible to the attacker. Prior work by Biggio et al \cite{biggio2013evasion} had introduced transfer attacks already, but granted access to such a dataset.

First, the substitute architecture is defined based on input and output of the target, e.g. one adapted to images. The architecture is overall not a deciding factor in the success of this approach.

The dataset restriction poses a difficulty for training the substitute. Papernot et al. solve this by querying the target on artificial data points to create a substitute dataset. Here, the selection of good data points to learn similar decision boundaries is crucial: a bad crafting strategy would require infeasible and highly suspicious amounts of queries to the target model. In particular, generation based on random noise does not work.

Instead, they rely on a heuristic to find out in which input direction the (substitute!) model's output changes and adding new samples focused on these directions. For that, they rely on the substitute model's Jacobian matrix (multidimensional derivative, each entry is one output coordinate partially derived by an input coordinate) at input points.

They start by collecting a set of samples somewhat representative of the target's input domain, such as different natural images.
Next, an architecture is selected.

The following steps are then performed repeatedly: The adversary makes the target model label his current selection of samples, and then trains his substitute with the new examples. Then, he augments his dataset with additional input points.

Let \(J_f(x)\) be the Jacobian of the substitute at a point x. Then, \(J_f(x)[o(x)]\) is the column corresponding to partial derivatives of the correct label (i.e. the one assigned by the target model). Papernot et al. now add the sign of this column scaled by a factor \(\lambda\) to point x: \(x = x + \lambda sign(J_f(x)[o(x)]\), and add it to their dataset.

By doing this to all current points, they obtain a larger sample set, so the model trained in the next iteration is closer to the target. Note that every epoch, the model is trained from scratch. With enough iterations, the substitute can then be used to generate the adversarial examples.

\subsubsection{Ensemble Attacks}
To improve transferability of adversarial examples created on substitute models, Liu et al. \cite{liu2016delving} argue that an adversarial example that works well for multiple models at the same time is bound to transfer better than one that only works on a single one.

That's why they propose that an adversary in the transfer attack setting crafts adversarial examples against an ensemble of models. For a targeted attack, they suggest the negative-log-likelihood-objective:

\[
x^{*} = \arg\min_{x'}{-\log((\sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i J_i(x'))*\mathbb{1}_{y^{*}}) + \lambda \lVert x - x'\rVert}
\]
where \(J(*)\) is each models output, \(\alpha_i\) scaling factors for each such that \(\sum_i\alpha_i=1\), for example simply \(1/k\).

\subsection{Clean-Label attacks}
Notably, while the backdoor poisoning attack described by Chen et al. does not need access to the model, the adversary has to be able assign arbitrary labels to samples, as does the one by Gu et al. However, this can be highly noticeable and alert human overseers to the attack or might just not be feasible in another way. Clean-label attacks  do not give such power to the adversary.

One such attack was proposed by Shafahi et al. \cite{shafahi2018poison}. In contrast to Chen et al.'s attack, the adversary does know the model parameters, which might happen if the target model is based on a pretrained, freely accessible one. His goal is to have a target instance misclassified without modifying it, e.g. getting a malware script to bypass a ML antivirus. 

First, he chooses a base instance, such that its class, the "base class", is the one he wants the target instance to be classified as. With this, he crafts a poison instance $p$ by solving 
\[

\]
where the first term compares the target t and poison candidates x in feature space (the more similar, the better), and the second penalizes difference from the base instance. For optimization, two steps are iterated: First, a gradient step ("forward step"), then a step towards b ("backwards step").

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Real-World and Physical Adversarial Attacks}

Somewhat surprisingly, in practice, confirmed \textit{malicious} adversarial-example attacks against real institutions or individuals remain strikingly rare.\cite{biggio2018wild, raff2023dont} Two explanations seem possible. 

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Adversarial attacks slip through unnoticed: a successful adversarial input may be logged as an ordinary misclassification, be considered a generic model error, or filed under an unrelated incident category, so that successfull employed adversarial attacks fly under the radar.
	\item Adversarial attacks might in practice simply be of little use to actual attackers. Crafting them reliably demands specialised machine-learning and security expertise, whereas cruder techniques---distributed denial-of-service, character obfuscation to slip past spam filters, or merely packing a malware binary---remain cheap, fast, and effective.\cite{apruzzese2023real, raff2023dont}
\end{enumerate}

 The second explanation is the one that we consider to be more plausible.  Building an adversarial example to defeat a system that a simpler attack would already defeat is like learning to pick a lock when the crowbar is cheaper, quicker, and works just as well. Interestingly, the few confirmed real-world cases, such as commercial CAPTCHA-solving services or the prompt injection of deployed chatbots, tend to be those where no such simpler alternative exists.

This does not render the phenomenon academic in the pejorative sense. As machine learning is embedded ever more deeply into the physical world---autonomous vehicles, automated border control, biometric surveillance---the question of whether an input that is semantically unchanged to a human can nonetheless fool a sensor pipeline becomes a central engineering concern. The body of work on \textit{physical} adversarial attacks shows that it can: inconspicuous stickers on a stop sign reliably cause targeted misclassification, both in the laboratory and from a moving vehicle;\cite{eykholt2018robust} printable adversarial patches force a chosen label onto almost any scene they appear in;\cite{brown2017adversarial} adversarial eyeglass frames defeat face recognition;\cite{sharif2016accessorize} and spoofed sensor returns or crafted road-surface markings can mislead the perception stack of a production self-driving system.\cite{cao2019adversarial, tencent2019tesla}

\subsection{Explaining Adversarial Examples}

Having seen \textit{where} adversarial examples arise, we now turn to \textit{why} they exist, highlighting some influential explanation attempts that have been proposed since their discovery in 2013. 

\paragraph{Mysterious Nonlinearity}
The initial paper by Szegedy et al. that introduced the concept of adversarial attacks, already made various core observations that shaped research ever since.\cite{szegedy2013intriguing} Firstly, they postulated that adversarial examples are neither random artefacts nor isolated anomalies: they occupy entire regions of the input space---\textit{pockets} in the data manifold---that are sparse, and therefore hard to encounter through random sampling, yet can be produced reliably through optimisation. Second, the effect is not merely overfitting. The same adversarial input often fools different models with different hyperparameters, and even different architectures---the phenomenon later called \textit{transferability}---which already argued against attributing adversarial examples to a single model overfitting its training set.

The missing piece that was not offered by their paper was an explanation of the curious phenomenon. Szegedy et al. do postulate that \textit{highly nonlinear model behaviour} might be behind this issue, but no comprehensive explanation was offered. It was only the next year that such explanation attempt was made. 

\paragraph{High-Dimensionality}
The explanation by Goodfellow et al.~\cite{goodfellow2014explaining} can be regarded as one of the most influential in the field. It overturns the intuition, inherited from \cite{szegedy2013intriguing}, that adversarial examples stem from the \textit{extreme non-linearity} of deep networks---highly contorted decision surfaces with rare, low-probability blind spots---possibly compounded by overfitting. Goodfellow et al.\ argued: linear behaviour in high-dimensional spaces is already \textit{sufficient} to produce adversarial examples; refuting the nonlinearity explanation attempt. Because modern networks are often times deliberately built to locally behave in near-linear ways (eg. using ReLu) for ease of optimisation, the argument carries over from strictly linear models to deep networks.

The mechanism is a high-dimensional \textit{bloating} effect. For a linear model with weight vector $w$, however, the response to a perturbed input $x+\eta$---the original input $x$ plus a perturbation $\eta$---is $w^\top(x+\eta) = w^\top x + w^\top\eta$, so the perturbation shifts the activation by $w^\top\eta$. The overall output change of a pertubation thus scales linearly with the number of input dimensions $n$ and mean weight magnitude $m$ with respect to the per-pixel change:
\[
w^\top\eta = \epsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert w_i\rvert = \epsilon\, m\, n.
\]
This means that in sufficiently high dimensions, many individually imperceptible, weight-aligned changes accumulate into one large change in the output. Figure~\ref{fig:linearity} illustrates this: the per-pixel budget stays flat as $n$ grows, while the change in the model's output climbs until it crosses the threshold that flips the prediction.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_linearity.pdf}
    \caption{The linear explanation of adversarial examples. Under an $L_\infty$ (per-pixel) budget every input coordinate is perturbed by at most $\epsilon$, so the perceptual size of the perturbation, $\lVert\eta\rVert_\infty = \epsilon$, stays fixed as the input dimension $n$ grows. The induced change in the model's activation, $w^\top\eta = \epsilon\,m\,n$, instead grows \textit{linearly} with $n$. Once the dimension is large enough (at $n^{*}$) this accumulated change crosses the threshold that flips the prediction---so in high dimensions an imperceptible perturbation suffices.}
    \label{fig:linearity}
\end{figure}

This point is easily overstated, and often is: Goodfellow et al.\ do not claim that linearity is a necessary ingredient of the explanation. They show only that non-linearity is not necessary---the simplest possible model, plain logistic or softmax regression with no hidden layer, already exhibits adversarial examples---as they showcased with FGSM which we discussed in section~\ref{sssec:fgsm}. (The refined, later view holds that both dimensionality and local curvature matter, as reflected in the stronger iterative and minimum-norm attacks discussed above.\cite{madry2017towards, moosavi2016deepfool})

The linear view also provided the first explanation of transferability. Since adversarial examples now occupy broad, contiguous half-spaces rather than fine pockets, different models' adversarial regions overlap heavily, which explains why an example crafted for one model tends to fool another at all. And because the discriminative direction is largely a property of the data rather than of the model, models trained on the same task tend to learn similar weight vectors, so that the same perturbation pushes them toward the same incorrect class---which explains why the models tend to agree on that class. 

\paragraph{The Fundamental Mismatch}
Ilyas et al.~\cite{ilyas2019adversarial} shift the framing once more, reframing adversarial examples not as pathological failures but as a \textit{mismatch between machine and human perception}: models exploit patterns that are genuinely predictive, but imperceptible to, and unintuitive for, humans. They distinguish \textit{predictive} features (features correlated with the label) from \textit{robust} features (features that remain predictive under perturbation). Since \textit{empirical risk minimisation} rewards any signal that improves accuracy, a learner will readily rely on predictive but non-robust features; adversarial vulnerability is then an expected consequence of standard training, rooted in the gap between the human sense of similarity and the statistical structure of the data. A striking experiment makes this concrete: they relabel every image of CIFAR-10 with a new, random target class and perturb it so that a model reads the new label, while to a human the images appear unchanged and hence entirely mislabelled. A standard model trained on this dataset nevertheless generalises well to the \textit{clean}, unmodified test set---evidence that it has latched onto real, transferable features that merely happen to be imperceptible to us. This view also bears on interpretability: if a model succeeds by relying on human-imperceptible features, there may be little in its decisions for a human to understand.

\subsection{Pertinence of Adversarial Examples as a Subject Area}

The two preceding subsections shed an interesting light on adversarial attacks as a field of study in the domain of cyber security. In the first, we asked whether they pose a real danger and found that little evidence can be found that adversarial attacks pose a pertinent security threat in any practical scenario. As a live security threat they have so far been marginal, dwarfed by cruder and cheaper forms of attack.\cite{apruzzese2023real, raff2023dont} Adding in our examination of different explanations for the phenomenon, we come to the conclusion that the primary significance of adversarial examples is of \textit{epistemic} nature. Their main impact can be seen as offering a new view on the inherent vulnerabilities of machine learning systems; shaking up pre-existing believes. In other words, their importance lies less in what they let an attacker do than in what they reveal about the systems they target.

What they reveal is considerable. Before the discovery and systematic study of adversarial examples, machine-learning models were evaluated almost exclusively on accuracy, and robustness was expected only implicitly.\cite{biggio2018wild, hendrycks2019benchmarking} Adversarial examples showed that high test accuracy does not imply that a model has grasped a learning task the way a human would: a classifier will confidently mislabel an input that is, to a human observer, semantically unchanged. This was the original observation that made the phenomenon of adversarial attacks so remarkable. This forced a more intricate account of how machine learners operate---from Goodfellow et al.'s high-dimensional linearity to Ilyas et al.'s reframing of adversarial features as genuine but human-imperceptible signal\cite{goodfellow2014explaining, ilyas2019adversarial}---and, with it, a redefinition of \textit{robustness} from an assumed by-product of accuracy into a distinct property that must be explicitly demanded, engineered, and evaluated. This shift is not merely academic: as machine learning is embedded in autonomous vehicles, border control, and surveillance, physical adversarial attacks make plain that robustness cannot be taken for granted in exactly those deployments where its absence would be most costly.

Viewed from a distance, the lesson generalises well beyond the field. When we trust a simple mathematical proxy to stand in for a messy real-world objective---as an overriding focus on accuracy does for machine learners---we are bound to be disappointed, and the resulting mismatch can surface suddenly and in unexpected ways. The contribution of adversarial examples is to have made that mismatch---between human and machine notions of similarity and understanding---impossible to ignore.
